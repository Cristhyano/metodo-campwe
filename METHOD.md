# The Campwe method · version 0.2

**Every city is the future of another.**

The solution to a city's problem most likely already exists, somewhere else or at another time. Progress spreads
unevenly across the world, so what one place still struggles with, another solved years ago, or centuries ago. The
Campwe method does two things:
1. **It finds solutions that already exist** and brings them to the people who need them.
2. **It adapts them with the community**, and builds new ones only when nothing fits.

The neighborhood names the problem, university students search, adapt, and build with the people who live it, and
the city tests the ones that advance. Everything stays public, credited to whoever made it, and any other city can
adapt it.

This is version 0.2, written before the first complete cycle. Anything not yet tested is marked **(to be tested)**.

---

## 1. The premise

The bow and arrow, the wheel, and agriculture each appeared in different places and at different times. When people
share a need and face similar conditions, their solutions tend to converge. That has two consequences:

- **Search before inventing.** Look for the need and the conditions, not for the place or a keyword.
- **Context decides adoption.** Mesoamerican peoples knew the wheel, in toys, but did not use it for transport:
  there were no draft animals, and the terrain did not help. A solution that exists elsewhere only works here if it is
  adapted to the conditions here, with the people who live them.

"The future" is not a fixed place. Every city is ahead on some problems and behind on others, so the exchange goes
both ways. Past solutions count as much as present ones.

---

## 2. Principles

1. **The door is open to everyone.** Anyone can raise a problem and follow what was done with it.
2. **The problem belongs to the neighborhood.** The challenge comes out of listening, never out of a ready-made agenda.
3. **The solution probably already exists.** Search other places and other times before inventing.
4. **The author moves the idea forward.** Nobody promotes or discards someone else's idea without saying why.
5. **Credit is recorded.** Every idea has an author, a date, an origin, and a history of who contributed what.
6. **Global problem, local solution.** An idea can be adapted to another place, and the adaptation always points back
   to its origin.
7. **Sponsors don't own ideas.** Companies and foundations support the cycle and receive the report, but the ideas
   stay public.
8. **Everything goes back to the street.** A cycle only ends when the neighborhood learns what happened to what it
   said.

---

## 3. The way of thinking: five steps

This is the part of the method meant to be repeated in any city, by anyone.

1. **Abstract.** Turn the neighborhood's problem into a universal need and the principle you are looking for, without
   naming the place. "The street floods every winter" becomes "drain water away fast, or hold it where it falls."
2. **Search in space and time.** Who had the same need under similar conditions, in another city today, in the past,
   or in a place that is "ahead" on this problem?
3. **Compare the conditions.** Climate, resources, materials, income, infrastructure, culture, governance, and scale.
   The closer the conditions, the more direct the transfer.
4. **Adapt with the community.** The principle stays; the local form is made here, to local standards, with the
   people who live the problem.
5. **Test and record.** The result becomes a new solution card, linked to its origin
   ([template](templates/solution-card.md)).

Every solution, found or new, is described by the same six fields: **need, principle, conditions, local form,
adoption, and coordinates** (where, when, and whether it arose on its own or came from elsewhere). Over time, the
cards form an atlas of solutions across space and time, and each city that joins makes the search better.

---

## 4. The building: three floors

The method is a path through three floors. What defines it is not each floor, but the passage from one to the next.

| Floor | What happens | Who is there | What moves up to the next floor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st · Listening** | The problem is told | Residents, local shops, neighborhood services | One clear question and the need behind it |
| **2nd · Challenge** | Existing solutions are found and adapted, and new ones emerge | Student teams with a faculty advisor, working with the people who live the problem | Solution cards with versions, origin, and credit |
| **3rd · Implementation** | The idea becomes a test and a project | Local government, sponsors, the idea's team | The test result, which goes back to the neighborhood |

---

## 5. Roles

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Cycle organizer** | Runs the listening, writes the challenge, operates the idea registry, organizes the final, and writes the report. Does not pick the ideas: looks after the path |
| **Educational institution** | Registers the challenge in its own system (in Brazilian universities, as an extension project, the community engagement every degree must include), appoints a coordinator and one faculty advisor per team, and certifies student hours under its own rules |
| **Local government** | Helps choose the neighborhood, names a contact person, provides space, and assesses with the organizer the test of the ideas that advance |
| **Residents** | Tell the problem, work with the teams, and say what they think of the ideas |
| **Teams** | Students from one or more programs and one or more institutions, each team with an advisor. Their first job is to search, not to invent |
| **Sponsor** (optional) | Pays part of the operation or the test. Receives the report and the contact details of those who agreed to be contacted. Owns no idea |

When several institutions join together, the cycle has a **committee** with one coordinator from each, the organizer,
and local government. The same neighborhood and the same challenge apply to all. Each institution certifies its own
students, and institutions are not ranked against each other.

---

## 6. The six-month cycle

The cycle fits in one academic semester, so the challenge fits into the universities' extension calendar.

| Month | Stage | What happens | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Listening** | Open meetings in the neighborhood. People who live and work there say what needs to change | The listening sheet, the challenge question, and the need behind it ([template](templates/listening.md)) |
| 2 | **Launch and search** | The challenge is published, institutions register the project, and teams form. Each team searches other places and times for solutions to the same need | The challenge sheet, with the final's criteria published in advance ([template](templates/challenge.md)), and each team's solution cards for what already exists |
| 3 | **Field and adaptation** | Teams go to the neighborhood with their advisors, compare conditions, and adapt with the people who live the problem. They invent only where nothing fits | Each team's field notes |
| 4 | **Ideas** | Ideas and their versions go public, each saying where it came from. The city supports them and suggests improvements | Published ideas, with author, origin, and history |
| 5 | **Final** | A final presentation open to the city, with attendance recorded | The list of ideas that move on to testing |
| 6 | **Report** | The public report states the outcome and the origin of each idea, and the ones that advanced move up to the 3rd floor | The cycle report ([template](templates/report.md)) |

Testing the ideas that advance happens after the cycle, with local government. It may take longer than one semester
**(to be tested)**.

---

## 7. The idea registry

Each idea is recorded as a project with a history:

| What a person does | What gets recorded |
|---|---|
| Writes the idea in one sentence | The idea is born, with author, date, and place |
| Says where it came from | The **origin**: the solution card it adapts, or "new" |
| Improves their own idea | A new version, with what changed |
| Proposes an improvement to someone else's idea | A proposal, which only the author accepts or declines |
| Adapts the idea to another place | A **derivative**, which points to the origin and never erases it |
| Supports it | A support, with a name |

Who contributed what, and where each idea came from, can be reconstructed at any time. That is what makes real
credit possible in the report and the certificate, including credit to the place and time a solution came from.

**People can withdraw their personal data.** The idea stays recorded, and the credit becomes anonymous.

---

## 8. The rules that don't change

They apply to every cycle and every version of the method. A new version may improve the path, never break these
rules.

1. **The problem belongs to the neighborhood.** The challenge comes out of listening.
2. **Ideas are public, with credit.** Nobody owns an idea from the challenge: not the organizer, not the sponsor,
   not the institution.
3. **No ranking of people and no large cash prize.** The final's criteria are agreed with the institutions and
   published in advance.
4. **Each party certifies what is theirs.** Extension hours are certified by the educational institution. The
   organizer certifies only what it measures: attendance at the final and contribution to ideas.
5. **Every idea has a declared outcome.** Adopted, adapted, or archived, and why.

---

## 9. The cycle report

The report is the proof that the cycle happened. It is public and states:

- who was heard during listening (how many people, from where, without exposing anyone who chose not to appear);
- the challenge question, the need behind it, and why it was chosen;
- the existing solutions the teams found, and where and when they came from;
- how many teams, programs, and institutions took part;
- the ideas and their versions, with each person's credit;
- who attended the final;
- **the outcome of each idea: adopted, adapted, or archived, and why**, and whether it was adapted from elsewhere or
  new;
- what changes in the next cycle.

## 10. Related methods

Almost every step has a well-known relative. What this method adds is the combination: closing the loop on every
idea, ideas that branch like code across cities, a steady source of participants through mandatory university
extension, and everything open.

- **TRIZ** (Genrich Altshuller, from the 1940s): inventive principles recur across unrelated fields, so abstract the
  problem, find the general principle, and come back to the concrete. The five steps apply this to cities.
- **A Pattern Language** (Christopher Alexander, 1977): recurring solutions to recurring problems, each described as
  problem, context, and solution. The solution card follows the same idea, written with the community.
- **Participatory action research and popular education** (Orlando Fals Borda and Paulo Freire, Latin America): the
  community owns the problem; it is not the object of study.
- **Design thinking and the Double Diamond:** understand, define a question ("How might we…"), develop, and deliver.
- **The scientific method:** listening is observation, the question is the research question, ideas are hypotheses,
  the final's criteria are set before the test, the neighborhood test is the experiment, the report publishes negative
  results too, and derivatives in other cities are replication.

## 11. How to adapt it for your city

This method is free (see the [license](LICENSE)). To run a cycle:

1. Choose a neighborhood and a problem with your local government.
2. Find an educational institution that will register the challenge in its system.
3. Follow the five steps (§3), the cycle (§6), and the [templates](templates/).
4. Publish the report and your solution cards.

When you adapt it, say where it came from: "based on the Campwe method," with a link to this repository. If you
change the method and distribute your version, it must keep the same license. Tell us what you changed: open an
issue in this repository or register your version in [DERIVATIVES.md](DERIVATIVES.md).

The Campwe name is not covered by the license. See [TRADEMARK.md](TRADEMARK.md).

## 12. What we don't know yet (to be tested in the first cycle)

- How many listening meetings are enough for a good question.
- How long teams need to search before they adapt or invent.
- How often an existing solution fits, compared with building a new one.
- Whether teams mixing students from different institutions work better or worse.
- How long the neighborhood test needs after the cycle.
- How much of the operation local government can take over on its own from the second cycle on.
